That separation is the main reason to trust the control. If import exposure were driving the concentration result, the two would move together. They do not: exposure does its work in 2024 and 2026, concentration only in 2026, and the giant gap opens and closes across 2025 while both others are flat.

Specification

log Pct = αc + δt + Σt βt(CR3z×1[t]) + Σt φt(Giant×1[t]) + Σt ψt(Exposurez×1[t]) + εct

284 categories over 31 months, 8,804 observations, January 2024 as reference, standard errors clustered by category, R² = 0.9953. No exchange rate enters the model — the earlier version interacted the terms with log(USD/ILS), which forces each gap to move in proportion to the currency and cannot represent a gap that opens and closes. The shekel panel above is context for reading the timing, not a regressor.

Import exposure is the category's input-cost currency exposure, weighted over its (manufacturer × category) pairs — a distinct thing from where goods are made, since rice packed locally is fully exposed while locally bottled cola is barely exposed. One standard deviation is 28 points of a 0–100 score.

Adding it changes almost nothing. CR3 moves from p = 0.141 to p = 0.162 and its endpoint from +1.65% to +1.62%; the giant term holds at p = 0.0003 with its endpoint at −0.14%. The control is real — jointly significant at p = 0.0053 — and still does not disturb them, which is the useful result.

What this still does not settle. Price is a unit value, so a shift in the mix of goods inside a category moves it with no shelf price changing, and a gap that opens and closes is exactly what mix drift would look like. The exposure measure rests on judged input cost shares, because the CBS input-output tables that would supply them are from 2014 and far coarser than 300 categories. And this is one currency episode in one country: the timing is suggestive, not identification.
